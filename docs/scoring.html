Scoring: add sound only where it matters

An English translation of a voice note, what "scoring" means in it, and how to use the idea.

English translation

Life has its corners. When you add small extra sounds to those corners, that's scoring. It doesn't have to be everywhere.

For example, say you're reading a book. The sound of a page turning is enough for us to understand that you're reading a book. And while you read, we don't need to hear the extra sounds of you touching the page or breathing.

(This is only an example.)

What "scoring" means here

Formally, a film's score is the music composed for it, and scoring is writing and placing that music. The speaker uses the word more loosely, for sound design: choosing the few sounds (small effects, foley, or short music cues) that you add to a scene to support the picture.

The core idea is selective sound. A scene has a handful of key moments (the "corners"). Put a small, clear sound on those moments and leave the rest alone. Silence, or just the natural room sound, is a valid choice everywhere else.

Sound design
Deciding which sounds the audience hears, and when.
Foley
Everyday sounds recorded or created to match actions on screen: footsteps, cloth, a page turning.
Sound cue
One short sound that tells the viewer something happened (a page turn means "reading").
Signal vs. noise
A sound that carries meaning is signal. Breath, finger-on-paper rustle and other incidental detail are usually noise.

The example: reading a book

Page turningKEEP: it says "reading"
Fingers touching the pageSKIP: adds nothing
Breathing while readingSKIP: distracting

One sound at the right moment tells the whole story. Adding the touch and the breath would not make the scene clearer. It would only make it busier, and it would bury the one sound that mattered.

Rules of thumb

Do
  • Find the moments that carry meaning: a cut, an action, a reveal.
  • Use one clear, recognizable sound per moment.
  • Keep it short and mix it under the voice.
  • Let silence or room tone fill the gaps.
Avoid
  • Sound on every movement "for realism".
  • Stacking several sounds on one action.
  • Sounds that mean nothing to the story (breath, rustle).
  • Effects that cover the words.

Test: remove a sound. If nobody would notice it missing, it was not scoring, it was clutter.

How this applies to the Kilo CLI Short

  • Sound effects are only on the key moments (the start, each scene change, the "critical part", the DeepSeek reveal), not on every on-screen movement.
  • Effects are mixed low (about 30%) and the music ducks to about 6% while someone is speaking, so the voice stays clear.
  • The voice recording is cleaned (rumble and hiss reduced) so incidental noise doesn't compete with the message.
  • The first 3 seconds have no voice. A small typing sound plus quiet music sets the scene without filling the space.
